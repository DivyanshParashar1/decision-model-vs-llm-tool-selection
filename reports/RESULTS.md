# Results: decision model vs fine-tuned LLM for tool selection

> Audience: anyone reviewing this experiment. Every number comes from [tables.md](tables.md) and
> [results.json](results.json), generated by `tools/report.py` from the per-instance prediction files.
> Design: [SPEC.md](../SPEC.md). Data: MetaTool, k = 10 candidate tools per request (gold + 4 TF-IDF look-alikes + 5 random).

## Summary

**After fine-tuning, the 486M decision model matches or beats the QLoRA fine-tuned Qwen3-8B on choosing one tool, and it
used about 3× fewer training GPU-hours.** It loses on requests that need two tools, which it was not trained for.

- **Tools seen in training:** fine-tuned GLiNER (C4) scores 0.979 ± 0.002 (mean ± sd over 3 seeds) and fine-tuned Qwen (C3)
  scores 0.951. GLiNER wins with every seed (McNemar p ≤ 3e-5).
- **Held-out tools, never seen in training:** C4 0.857 ± 0.008 vs C3 0.857. That is a tie (p = 0.17–0.8 across seeds).
  Both models were trained on the same 10,897 requests and see these 30 tools only through their descriptions.
- **Requests that need two tools:** C3 wins, 0.708 vs 0.645 ± 0.014 for the exact pair (p = 0.0002–0.02).
- **Cost:** training C4 at full size took 0.86 T4-hours per seed. C3 took 2.62. At inference, C4 needs about 110 ms per
  decision on one T4. C3 needs about 500 ms per decision on two T4s, even with batching (see Cost for the caveat).
- **Without fine-tuning, the result is reversed.** Raw Qwen3-8B (C1) beats raw GLiNER (C2) by 9 points on seen tools and
  11 on unseen tools (p < 1e-15), and they tie on the official prompts. The decision model is only competitive once it is
  fine-tuned.

- C4 starts about 9 points behind but is ahead from the first 1k examples onward. Fine-tuning moves the small model much
  more (+21 points on seen tools) than the 8B LLM (+9).

  Generalizing to new tool descriptions levels off before accuracy on seen tools does.

## 4. Cost

Training GPU-hours are on one Tesla T4.

| | C3 Qwen3-8B QLoRA | C4 GLiNER fine-tune |
|---|---|---|
| Parameters | 8B (4-bit base); 43.6M trainable LoRA (r16) | advertised 340M; **486M as loaded**, all trainable |
| Train GPU-h at 1k / 4k / full | 0.26 / 1.09 / 2.62 | 0.08 / 0.32 / 0.86 (mean of 3 seeds) |
| Training recipe | 1 epoch, batch 8 × 2 | 3 epochs, batch 8 |
| Peak training VRAM | 8.7 GB | 15.4 GB (almost the full T4) |
| Inference | ~500 ms/decision, batched, 2× T4 (vLLM TP=2, LoRA adapter) | ~110 ms/decision unbatched (median 109–132 ms), 1× T4 |
| Total for this study | 3.97 GPU-h (3 sizes × 1 seed) | 3.77 GPU-h (3 sizes × 3 seeds) |

Inference caveat: the raw Qwen (C1) ran at 172 ms per decision under the same vLLM setup. About two-thirds of C3's
inference time is the cost of serving an unmerged LoRA adapter on T4. Merging the adapter would bring C3 to about the C1
speed. Even then it needs two GPUs, where GLiNER needs one.

## 5. Error analysis

**Hard distractors.** Most errors, for every config, pick one of the 4 TF-IDF-similar tools rather than one of the 5 random
ones. That share is 61% (C2) to 88% (C3) of errors on test_seen, and 89–92% for the fine-tuned models on test_unseen. Both
arms fail in the same place: tools whose descriptions overlap, such as two search tools or two travel tools.

**Tool-name leak.** About 5% of queries contain the gold tool's name. Accuracy without them is within 0.01 of the overall
number for every config (for example C4 s0 0.979 vs 0.980, C3 0.948 vs 0.951). Name matching does not explain the results.

**Two-tool requests.** Fine-tuned GLiNER's top pick is one of the two gold tools 89–90% of the time, about the same as C3's
88%. It loses on the second pick. GLiNER was trained only on single-tool examples with a softmax over tools, and we take
its top 2. Its multi-tool score also varies a lot by seed at 1k (0.557–0.652). The LLM is simply asked for two tools.
Neither arm saw two-tool training data, so this is a gap in the method, not a fundamental limit.

**Calibration.** GLiNER's confidence is well calibrated after fine-tuning: ECE on test_seen falls from 0.11 (raw) to
0.02 (full). If it answers only its 90% most-confident decisions, accuracy is 0.994–0.998. The LLM gives no comparable
confidence score here.

## 6. Limitations

- **One seed for the LLM arm** (GPU budget). The C4 vs C3 gaps on test_seen and test_multi are larger than C4's
  seed-to-seed spread, but C3's own seed variance is unmeasured.
- **No hyperparameter search** for either arm. Each used one standard recipe (QLoRA r16, lr 1e-4, 1 epoch; GLiNER
  encoder lr 1e-5, task lr 5e-4, 3 epochs). The epoch counts differ by design (SPEC §5). A 2–3 epoch LLM\* might close
  some of the seen-tool gap at higher cost.
- **Label noise was not measured.** SPEC §7 planned a manual check of 100 errors made by both arms; it was not done. Some
  MetaTool queries are vague ("How can I make this immersive?"), so part of the remaining error is probably label noise.
- **One benchmark, one candidate format.** k = 10 with TF-IDF distractors. Results on harder distractors (for example,
  embedding-based ones) or longer tool lists may differ.
- **The official MetaTool comparison is partly contaminated by train overlap.** Section 1 reports the overlap-free subset.
- **C5 (LLM shortlist → decider) was not run.** SPEC made it conditional on C4 < C3, which did not happen for single tools.

## 7. How this relates to the chess experiment

In chess, the fine-tuned decider lost when it had to pick from the LLM's candidates (26.8% vs 36.1% top-1) because
good moves need lookahead and the LLM's lists often missed the best move. Tool selection removes both problems: the options
are given and the decision is shallow. Here the fine-tuned decider wins on single-tool selection at roughly a third of
the training compute. That supports the hypothesis from the chess write-up: decision models work well when the choice
set is given and judging each option needs little reasoning.
